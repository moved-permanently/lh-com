["Why is tomato juice so popular on airplanes? And what do the beeps mean? We answer 5 questions travelers often ask themselves when flying.","Why do the window shades have to be raised during takeoff and landing? Why does tomato juice taste better on a plane – and what do those mysterious “beeps” on board actually mean? We answer the questions you’ve probably asked yourself while on a plane At home, everyone can eat whatever they like. But during the flight, the cockpit crew never eats the same thing. That's actually mandatory. The goal is to minimize the risk of both pilots getting food poisoning at the same time. The inconspicuous beeps like “ping” or “ding-dong” on board an airplane are much more than mere background noise: though often barely noticeable to passengers, they serve as a communication system for the cabin crew. They signal, for example, that the aircraft has reached its cruising altitude or that the seatbelt signs can be turned off. Or that the flight attendants should go to the nearest intercom speaker, which are located in the front and rear sections of the aircraft cabin. Either because the cabin crew wants to communicate internally, or, for example, because the pilot needs something. They also sound shortly before landing so that the flight attendants can make all the necessary preparations. Tomato juice above the clouds: because our sense of taste is altered, it tastes fruitier and more aromatic (© Johannes Mink; header image © Johannes Mink) Once the plane has reached cruising altitude, the air pressure in the cabin is lower and the air is relatively dry. This alters our sense of smell and taste, making it harder for us to perceive sweet and salty flavors. However, we can still taste the savory, umami flavor of tomato juice very well. In addition, tomato juice tastes fruitier, more refreshing, and less earthy above the clouds. These rules are part of international aviation regulations to ensure safety during takeoff and landing. The window shades must be raised so that cabin crew can better assess the situation outside in an emergency. It also allows emergency responders to see inside more clearly from the outside. Reclined seats could also unnecessarily hinder an evacuation. Dimming or turning off the cabin lights during takeoff and landing serves a similar purpose: the emergency lighting that marks the path to the exits is much more visible in the dark. An upright seatback keeps the escape route clear during takeoff and landing (© Johannes Mink) On long-haul flights, cabin crew are required to take set rest periods. While passengers doze or watch movies, the flight attendants take turns retreating to hidden sleeping compartments. These crew rest compartments are located in different parts of the plane depending on the aircraft type. In the Airbus A350 or the Boeing 787 Dreamliner, they are often located in the upper part of the fuselage. In the Airbus A380, however, they are on the lower deck near the cargo holds. Inside, flight attendants find a surprisingly cozy, albeit rather compact environment: narrow berths, separated from one another by curtains, ensure privacy, and safety belts keep sleepers securely in their berths even during turbulence. There is also a wardrobe where they can hang their uniforms. What’s the easiest way to check in? Only when I arrive in Tokyo early in the morning do I sleep for two hours first, otherwise I won’t last until the evening. In very hot places, jet lag can actually be quite useful if you use the sleeplessness to experience the city at night. In Asia, I like to go for a foot massage. It’s incredibly relaxing and revitalizing at the same time.” Preventing Jet Lag: Before and During Your Trip Start adjusting your sleep and meal timings to the time zone of your destination two to three days before your trip. This will help your biorhythm cope with the change. Get enough sleep before your flight. A sleep deficit will exacerbate jet lag symptoms after arrival. If possible, choose flights that arrive in the evening. This will allow you to sleep shortly after and adjust more quickly. Start adapting your daily rhythm to the destination time while still on the plane. Sleep on the plane if it is nighttime at your destination. A sleep mask, earplugs, and comfortable clothing can help. Drink water regularly during the flight. It is best to avoid alcohol and caffeine, as they can affect the quality of your sleep. Successful people in particular like to emphasize that time is their ultimate luxury, or the freedom to say no. However, this privilege is in turn linked to a certain economic autonomy. Nevertheless, just because something is expensive doesn't mean it deserves the title of luxury. Nowadays, the term luxury is attached to all kinds of things: hotels, spas, cosmetics, and the most banal accessories, which increasingly rarely deserve the label. As a result, the term has degenerated into a marketing formula – and one with a bad reputation, because luxury is associated with ostentation. Much of what we define as a luxury product today has long since been mass-produced. There is no other explanation for the billions in sales generated by the so-called luxury groups. The concept is as simple as it is problematic: goods of all kinds are enriched with culture, history and meaning, and so-called experts confirm their authenticity and determine their presumed collector's value. And then you have a modern luxury object, at least from a capitalist perspective. The craft behind it no longer determines the value. Particularly talented counterfeiters now produce superfakes that even specialists regularly fail to produce. So a prominently placed label does not equal luxury. Professor Lambert Wiesing is the first humanities scholar to venture a philosophical explanation.
