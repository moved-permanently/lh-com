["To mark its 100th anniversary, Lufthansa showcases how its iconic uniforms have evolved over seven generations.","Uniforms for flight attendants are first and foremost workwear – but they also reflect the spirit of their time and tell a piece of aviation history. In Lufthansa’s 100th anniversary year, the tradition continues with the seventh generation of the uniform. There's no better time to look back at the history of the workwear worn by Lufthansa flight attendants Flight attendants have worn uniforms from the very beginning. Stewardesses and stewards – as they were still called until the 1990s – quickly became style icons and the embodiment of cosmopolitan elegance. \"But uniforms are much more than just fashion; they're also workwear. They reflect social ideals and convey the company’s brand values,\" explains Anna-Christina Sauer, who is responsible for uniform management at Lufthansa. According to Sauer, there have been three constants in the uniform design since Lufthansa resumed operations in 1955. \"First, there is the color navy blue – the shade travelers naturally associate with the airline and one that conveys a sense of security both on the ground and in the air.\" The second is yellow, the brand’s signature color. The third constant is some form of headwear, which has appeared in different styles over the decades. \"Since 2002, the current design has featured a pillbox hat,” Sauer explains. “It’s optional, but it reflects the timeless chic of the Lufthansa uniform.\" Inspired by the elegant couture of Christian Dior: Lufthansa uniforms from 1955 onward The flight attendants are happy to take care of even the youngest passengers. In this case, after a long-haul flight in 1958 The captain and crew looking relaxed on the first Lufthansa flight to Prague in April 1968 Since 2002, the designs have been created by Strenesse. The fashion label embraces a kind of retro-futurism: dynamic, approachable, and elegant. The uniforms and accessories were designed by the brand’s legendary founder and lead designer, Gabriele Strehle. \"It is the sixth generation of uniforms since 1955,\" explains Patrizia Hey from Lufthansa Airlines Communications. She is very familiar with Lufthansa’s history, having worked for many years in the airline's archives, and she can reveal that a seventh generation will be introduced in the near future. In Lufthansa’s early years, only one man worked on board the aircraft. It wasn’t until 1938 that additional flight attendants, including women, joined to take care of passengers. What they wore had to be practical, yet elegant: the in-flight uniform consisted of a navy skirt, a white blouse paired with a dark tie or bow, and a light linen jacket with gold buttons. The Lufthansa crane emblem also proudly appeared in gold on the navy cap. Still stylish: two classics from past Lufthansa collections The first uniforms after Lufthansa resumed operations in 1955 were designed by Uli Richter. In his designs, the Berlin couturier avoided strict formality, drawing inspiration from the influential postwar fashion of the Parisian giant Christian Dior. A navy suit was paired with a white blouse and a jockey-style cap. For hot summer days, an alternative was the petrol-blue \"tropical suit\": a light blue dress with a matching cap. Ten years later, it was a woman’s turn to design clothing for the roughly 700 stewardesses and stewards of the time – and she knew exactly what a uniform required. Ursula Tautz was the former chief stewardess and had already advised Lufthansa on fashion for several years. Her designs focused on achieving the perfect balance between formal correctness and relaxed elegance. The gangway became a catwalk: uniforms with ultra-short skirts were all the rage in the early 1970s In 2002, German fashion icon Gabriele Strehle designed the uniforms for Lufthansa 1979: for the first time, flight attendants were also given the option of wearing a pantsuit In 1970, the next generation of the uniform was designed by Werner Machnik, another Berliner who had gained a reputation early in his career as the city’s youngest couturier. Machnik brought a new dynamism to the Lufthansa uniform. For nine years, flight attendants wore his designs: short suits – while not especially practical for flight attendants, the mini skirt was still very much in vogue – combined with cuts whose geometric structure was reminiscent of the French designer André Courrèges. The most striking piece from this era was a bright yellow cape with a blue lining. Toward the end of the 1970s, the look became noticeably more conservative. At this time, starting in 1979, female flight attendants were given the option of wearing pants as an alternative. A few statistics from that era illustrate the scale of the collection: the basic uniform set consisted of 33 items. Around 33,000 meters of navy fabric were used for coats, jackets, skirts, and pants, along with 9,000 meters for service dresses, 45,000 meters for blouses, and 3,000 meters for scarves. \"Accessories such as flight pins, pocket squares, scarves, and ties help make the uniform recognizable. They also allow for a slightly more playful approach to the uniform.\" Anna-Christina Sauer, Uniform Management From 1987 onward, there was a stronger focus on accessories such as the neck slip – a tie-like scarf – and both female and male flight attendants were allowed to wear rank insignia. \"Accessories such as the wings pin, pocket squares, scarves, and ties help make the uniform recognizable,\" Sauer explains. \"They also allow for a slightly more playful approach to the uniform.\" Shoes are not part of Lufthansa’s basic uniform kit, Sauer adds: \"Casual shoes are not permitted. With the uniform, classic shoes made of black or navy smooth leather are worn to maintain the professional business look.\" Despite the wide variety of colors and cuts over the decades, functionality is always key, emphasizes Anna-Christina Sauer. \"We have very complex requirements for our fabrics, particularly when it comes to performance, freedom of movement, and durability. In other words: how well can I move in them on board? Can I stretch? ["Tino Klähne from the Lufthansa Innovation Hub talks about smart cabins, in-flight internet, seamless travel, biometrics, and Cabinblu.","How will we travel in the future? We asked Tino Klähne from the Lufthansa Innovation Hub this question. Our conversation covers smart cabins, seamless travel, and realistic visions of the future Lufthansa Insights: Mr. Klähne, when you look into the future of flying – are we talking about a radical upheaval or a series of small steps? Tino Klähne: We're definitely talking about an evolution, not a revolution. In 15 or 25 years, an aircraft will still look the same as it does today: fuselage, wings, cabin, engines. The big leaps – like Concorde back in the day – are extremely difficult to implement in terms of financing and regulations. What is changing massively are the digital services and experiences around the flight. What do you think are the main drivers of change? Two things: the aircraft cabin and connectivity. As an airline, we are heavily reliant on what new aircraft Airbus, Boeing, and other manufacturers deliver. At the same time, we are seeing a trend toward retrofitting, i.e. modernizing existing cabins more quickly with new seats, better power supply, holders for tablets, and more charging points. The real game changer, however, is stable, fast internet on board. With Starlink, for example, Wi-Fi will be available in the Lufthansa Group in the future and the quality will be the same as what you're used to at home – and it will be free of charge for guests. When it comes to the future of flying, the term smart cabin is often used. What does this mean for me as a passenger? The smart cabin is basically a networked cabin that forms a platform, which means that your own devices can be easily connected to the system, for example. Content and services are personalized, from entertainment to food and seating preferences. In addition, relevant travel information is communicated proactively to the passenger: \"Your connecting flight is secured, gate XY, boarding postponed by 20 minutes\" – directly on the screen or in the app, even before landing. For the crew, there is more contextual information in the background: who is afraid of flying? Who is traveling with three small children? Who needs help with connecting flights? Ideally, the cabin feels a bit like a Tesla at 39,000 feet: digital features can be added via software updates without having to reconfigure half the aircraft. Personalization quickly starts to sound like annoying advertising. Will the airplane become a flying billboard? The topic of retail media is indeed big in the industry – in other words, personalized advertising in in-flight entertainment. Technically, many things are possible, including AI-generated ads in real time. But the balance is crucial: we can also use the same data to improve services – for example by showing the crew who has special needs, who we should prioritize assisting. The goal is not to commercialize the cabin, but to make traveling even more enjoyable. To what extent will the entire journey – from getting out the taxi to reaching the gate – be digitally interlinked? Much more so than today – the goal must be for it all to be seamless. Some airports are already measuring waiting times at security and check-in and are starting to share this data. In the future, travelers could receive early messages such as: \"If you get out of the taxi in 18 minutes, you can get through security and boarding in a relaxed manner.\" In addition, there could be links with taxis, ridesharing services, and hotels. If a flight is delayed, ideally your driver already knows that. And in the medium term, biometrics will simplify the journey through the airport, including duty free shopping. The key technology here is \"Pay by face,\" which means you don't need money or a card, but instead your face is scanned and the amount debited from your account. Lufthansa has also been testing various biometric solutions for several years. Technology is one thing. What do you personally want in the future as a traveler? Above all, fewer surprises. I no longer want to have to click through parking information, luggage rules, seating plans, and special processes, but to be able to trust that solutions will be proposed at an early stage in the event of unexpected situations. Internally, we like to call this \"certainty of success\": a high degree of trust that the trip will happen without me having to control everything myself. What is the Lufthansa Innovation Hub doing specifically to make the flying experience even better for travelers? We are already helping people with a fear of flying through the Cabinblu app, which we launched in 2025. About 40 percent of passengers are afraid of flying and fly anyway. For this reason, we have developed digital support with the help of a leading psychologist – with explanations, exercises, and meditations. In the future, something like this could also be integrated into Lufthansa apps and systems, including a notification to the crew or the cockpit so they can react more sensitively. And what do you currently have in the pipeline? We are working on several projects, often together with teams from the Lufthansa Group. Most of them concern internal improvements. As far as travelers are concerned, we are currently digitalizing processes for people with special needs – from wheelchair users to families with small children, among other things. One aim is to coordinate special service requests such as wheelchair assistance more easily. Our goal is for there to be fewer exceptions, greater accessibility as standard, and less stress for everyone involved. Another field is AI for ground operations. At the moment, gate agents have to perform many complex steps in parallel, whereas we are working on solutions to automate routine tasks so that our gate employees have more time for real guest interaction. What about the really big leaps – hypersonics, air taxis, beaming? I'm afraid neither of us will live to see the beaming (laughs). We have evaluated topics such as supersonic or hypersonic aircraft, but in order to operate them economically, we would need to charge so much for tickets that most people would say: \"That's too much money just to save two hours.\" Air taxis will play a role in niche areas, but because of security, infrastructure, and connections, they will be less of a solution to the basic problem of overall travel time than some hope. All of this means that the future of flying will not be spectacularly reinvented overnight.
