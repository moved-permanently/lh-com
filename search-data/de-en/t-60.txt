Join us on a journey through Saudi Arabia's capital and see Riyadh through Al-Neb's eyes Riyadh's rhythm is hard to define – a mix of heat, light, and ambition. This is a city that doesn’t wait for change; it builds it. I grew up here, watching its skyline rise one tower at a time, not realizing how much it was shaping me, too. After years abroad, I see Riyadh not just as my hometown, but as a place of transformation – where memory and modernity move together. Al Masmak: The Fortified Cradle of Saudi Arabia The story of modern Saudi Arabia began at Al Masmak Fortress, built in 1865. It was here that the future King Abdulaziz reclaimed Riyadh in 1902, uniting the kingdom. For me, it's also where my own story began. The plaza outside its walls is where I learned to walk, to fall, and to try again. Standing before the fortress today, I see more than history. Murabba Palace: A Place of Reconciliation Murabba Palace and its park are my favorite places to photograph. Its proportions feel human, measured, and graceful. When I stand there with my camera, I think of how much Riyadh has transformed, yet how its soul – this balance between simplicity and purpose – still lingers. It was the subject of my first photographs, my first attempt to see Riyadh through an artist's eye. Every return feels like a reconciliation between the version of me that left and the one who finally learned to truly see. Riyadh's Carpet Markets: Connecting Patterns The carpet markets are some of the most poetic places in Riyadh, a tapestry of color, craft, and conversation. In our culture, carpets are more than decoration; they are the ground of gathering, prayer, and storytelling. Walking through the market feels like traveling across regions. Saudi, Turkish, Afghan, Uzbek, and Indian designs coexist in harmony, each pattern carrying traces of its maker's world. Together they form a visual dialogue, threads connecting tribes, histories, and homes. I often trace the weaves with my fingertips, reminded that art, like memory, is strongest when shared. Each pattern feels like a version of the city itself: layered, patient, quietly alive beneath the surface. Riyadh: A City of Contrasts To see Riyadh through my eyes is to see a city alive in contrast: old walls beside glass towers, silence beside construction noise, the past breathing through the future. Riyadh has taught me to take a closer look and find beauty in persistence. Every place, from Al Masmak to Murabba, from Souq Al Zal to the carpet markets, tells me the same thing: growth is not about erasing what came before, but carrying it forward with grace. A declaration of love to a city full of light and shadow that has shaped Kganye The volume and vibrancy of Johannesburg inspires Lebohang Kganye, who grew up in the metropolis of millions. \"I can hardly imagine living anywhere else,\" says the multimedia artist. \"I love the energy of the city. It's incredibly fast-paced and sometimes even overwhelming.\" That's why Kganye also needs quiet moments and places where she can calm down. \"I always find little islands of tranquility in and around the city,\" she says. \"For example, in the small town of Magaliesburg, around 45 minutes from Johannesburg city center, or on a hike.\" She recommends the Suikerbosrand and Cradle Moon nature reserves and the Kings Kloof Trails hiking area for a break on the outskirts. Johannesburg's Excellent Food Scene Despite her strong ties to Johannesburg, Kganye spends four to six months abroad every year, in art capitals such as Paris and New York. When she returns, she looks forward to two things in particular: her beautiful apartment and the local food scene. It is diverse, often excellent, and surprisingly affordable. \"My favorite place at the moment is Obscura,\" says Kganye. \"The restaurant and cocktail bar is small and super intimate.\" When there, you absolutely have to order the roasted broccoli with sumac, honey, almonds, chili oil, crispy sage, red onions, and a Cape Malay curry velouté. \"The 44 Stanley shopping arcade feels more like a small village. With pretty boutiques, bookshops, and small, independent stores.\" Lebohang Kganye A very typical Johannesburg tradition is the \"braai,\" a barbecue held at car washes. This tradition is called \"shisanyama,\" which is a Zulu word that literally means \"burnt meat,\" and it is mainly celebrated in the townships. For tourists, Kganye recommends restaurants within the city to try authentic shisanyama, such as Kyalami Shisanyama or Chaf Pozi. Another one of Kganye's favorites is Kolonaki, a Greek restaurant. \"I think the ambience is beautiful. And the food is excellent – I always end up ordering too much,\" says Kganye. For Kganye, a perfect Sunday starts with coffee and a pastry at Test Bakery, followed by a shopping trip at the 44 Stanley shopping arcade. \"It feels more like a small village. With pretty boutiques, bookshops, and small, independent stores.\" Here, you'll also find the Library of Things We Forgot to Remember, an archive of records and art related to Black resistance movements in Africa. Three Favorite Tips 1) Book a treatment in the spa at The Westcliff hotel 2) Shopping for fashion at Boys of Soweto 3) Let yourself drift along Vilakazi Street in Soweto Kganye's art can be found in the collections of some of the world's most important museums, but she prefers to describe herself as a storyteller rather than an artist. This may also be due to the fact that as a teenager she dreamed of becoming an author and writing plays. At the time, she thought you had to study journalism to do that. She knew nothing about subjects such as creative writing or African literature. When her application to journalism school was unsuccessful, she took a course in photojournalism at the Market Photo Workshop photography school. \"My plan was to do that for a year and then concentrate on writing again,\" recalls Kganye. \"But then I fell in love with photography.\" At the Market Photo Workshop, founded in 1989 by South African photographer David Goldblatt, many learned the craft in order to document the brutality of the apartheid regime. With the end of state-imposed racial segregation, the students' interests and subjects also changed. Topics such as identity and homeland came to the fore. This was also the case for Kganye. \"My art deals with oral tradition, my roots, and my family history,\" she says. In particular, her family name Kganye, the Sesotho word for light, became the focus of her interest. \"In our culture, there is a tradition of 'praise poems' that consist of the clan names of our ancestors and thus become a chronicle of our own ancestry,\" says Kganye. Johannesburg's Turbulent History and Vibrant Creative Scene Johannesburg, family history, light – these are the themes of Kganye's life. \"For me, photography is writing with light,\" she says. According to Kganye, anyone who wants to get to know the light and dark sides of South Africa's history should visit Constitution Hill. Nelson Mandela was once briefly imprisoned there, as was Mahatma Gandhi, who fought against discrimination against Indian immigrants in South Africa before rebelling against the British colonial empire in his native India. Today, South Africa's Constitutional Court oversees civil rights there, and a museum brings visitors closer to South Africa's eventful history. \"Concerts are also held there,\" says Kganye. \"Especially on public holidays such as Freedom Day on April 27, which have a connection to the country's history.\" \"In Johannesburg, you can realize dream projects.\" Lebohang Kganye If Kganye wants to be inspired by works by up-and-coming artists, especially from Africa, she visits the BKhz Gallery. \"It was founded by the artist Banele Khoza and, unlike most other galleries, doesn't seem to focus solely on the art market.\" When it comes to art, another must-see for Kganye is the Joburg Contemporary Art Foundation. The brick building used to house a streetcar depot and now hosts temporary exhibitions. Kganye finds Johannesburg's creative scene to be very collaborative.
