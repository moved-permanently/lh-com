["In the age of meta cities and comprehensive digital networking, according to bestselling author and urban researcher Richard Florida, the need for personal encounters is even growing.","Richard Florida lets his thoughts take flight: in this age of Meta Cities and digital connectivity, the need to meet face-to-face has only intensified, writes the best-selling author and urbanist Right now, you’re likely reading this on a plane, as you wing your way to a destination that is hundreds or thousands of miles away from your home. Maybe you’re a businessperson who lives in Lisbon and works for a company in London. You visit with clients in Paris and Amsterdam regularly and with your colleagues in London once or twice a month. Or you’re on your way to a much-needed vacation in Miami. Remember just a few years ago when business travel was written off for dead? Why travel when you can Zoom, or so the story went. Time after time, pundits have predicted that this or that world-shrinking communication technology – the telephone, the fax, the Internet – would render business travel obsolete. And every time, they’ve gotten it wrong. By the end of 2023, global travel had surpassed its pre-pandemic levels. That’s because we humans love to connect with each other. In fact, we require it. A good deal of business is best done face-to-face. And of course, we just like to meet people and experience new places. Paradoxically, the more technology enables us to live and work apart, the greater our need to connect. Consider this: though far fewer workers are going to their offices, the urban centers of major cities like New York, London and Paris are surging back. Why? Because of visitors – not just tourists, but businesspeople who come to them to meet, collaborate and do deals. Visitors account for more than 60 percent of the activity in the downtowns of US cities, according to a recent study, while workers and residents combined make up just 40 percent. \"Travel gives us the best of all worlds, enabling us to live in a place we love, work in places we are most productive, and play in places where we can have the most fun.\" Richard Florida Richard Florida knows why traveling will not go out of fashion in the future (© Daria Malysheva; header image © Daria Malysheva) Driving all of this is the rise of what I call Meta Cities, a new type of city which combines digital and physical connectivity. Like the hub-and-spoke systems of airports, great global cities have eclipsed their physical boundaries and borders, and now function as veritable hubs of much farther-flung networks. The spokes that connect these hubs to the satellite cities in their orbit may cross different time zones and even continents, but taken together, they form coherent wholes that are defined by the ever-greater flows of people between them. Some of those hubs and satellites are specialized for work, some for living, and some for entertainment. The biggest and most important, like London and New York, provide all three; they are cities of everything. But the most rapidly rising Meta Cities – places like Dubai, Singapore, and Miami – have bolstered their global trajectories by investing in world-class airports, hotels and entertainment attractions. The very existence of these cities of connection turns on travel. We are social animals after all. If digital technology has enabled us to live and work in more places, it has massively increased our need to connect and collaborate in person. Travel gives us the best of all worlds, enabling us to live in a place we love, work in places we are most productive and play in places where we can have the most fun. This new age of connectivity also benefits society writ large, as greater connectivity between places spurs greater innovation, bolsters productivity and adds to our overall economic potential. ["Lufthansa flight attendant Marie Hecker shares how to join the cabin crew, from the application and training to requirements and life on board.","Flight attendant Marie Hecker shares what it takes to pursue a career 35,000 feet above the ground, reflects on her experience navigating the application process, and explains why, even after 14 years on the job, she still can't imagine a better career The chance to travel the world and see places you might never visit on your own is what draws many aspiring flight attendants to the job. I have to admit, though, that I had never seriously considered it myself. After graduating from high school, I asked friends and family what kind of career they could picture me pursuing. Then one friend said, \"I know two people who are flight attendants at Lufthansa – you'd be a great fit.\" Without expecting too much to come of it, I submitted an application to Germany's largest airline. “Apply – it’s the best job in the world.” Marie Hecker What I didn’t know at the time was that becoming a flight attendant at Lufthansa is not something that happens overnight. The job comes with a specific set of requirements – from strong German and English skills and a height between 1.55 and 1.95 meters (between 5'1\" and 6'5\") to solid general knowledge of the world. Just as important are the soft skills: a high degree of flexibility, openness and ability to adapt to constantly changing crews, people, and situations – and above all, perseverance. Crew members can sometimes be awake for up to 30 hours at a time, as working days can be long and demanding. Young and optimistic, I was confident that my experience working in hospitality during my school years had prepared me to meet those requirements. Then the application process began: an online application, an English test, and finally an invitation to the assessment center in Frankfurt. The most exciting part was the psychological evaluation, designed to determine whether candidates were suited to the responsibility of carrying out duties in the cabin. This was followed by the medical assessment and a background check conducted by the aviation security authorities. I received a job offer, and a few months later I began my training at Lufthansa Aviation Training in Schwaig, Bavaria. Unlike many other professional training programs, the theoretical portion of flight attendant training is considerably shorter and more intensive, lasting just three months. Divided into two sections, the first part focused entirely on onboard service – something that came naturally to me. Marie Hecker has been a Lufthansa flight attendant for 14 years (© Yves Bachmann/Lufthansa) Even on long workdays, friendly and attentive service is always part of the job (© Yves Bachmann/Lufthansa) Even before her training, Hecker gained valuable experience for onboard service while working in the restaurant industry (© Felix Wittich/Lufthansa) The second part was entirely new to me – and far more demanding. In the emergency training module, we learned how to handle safety procedures and a wide range of potential emergency situations. The evacuation drills in the aircraft mockup were both thrilling and challenging: we learned how to evacuate an entire aircraft within 90 seconds. Our instructors left us with an image that I have never forgotten: “You have to make the mental shift – from elegant pumps to safety shoes.” Learning to change our tone in critical situations as well – from “Could you please…” to “Do it!” – was one of the most powerful lessons of the entire training program. There is a certain pressure throughout the training, knowing that you can be dismissed at any point if you do not meet the required standards. Fortunately, almost everyone in our group made it through. At the end, we proudly held our certificates in our hands. My adventure as a flight attendant could finally begin. All of this took place around 15 years ago, and much has changed since then. Today, far more steps in the process are conducted digitally: parts of the training are completed online in advance, and the traditional assessment center has been replaced by more modern formats, such as casting sessions. One of Marie Hecker's first familiarization flights took her to San Francisco – and sparked her passion for travel (© Getty Images) Finally, I stepped on board for the first time as a fully qualified flight attendant – at least on paper. The reality was that I still had two familiarization flights ahead of me as part of the training, during which I benefited from a little extra support as a newcomer. I barely remember those first flights; I was running on pure adrenaline. And it quickly became clear that the reality of the job was very different to what we had learned in theory. What I will never forget, however, is that one of those familiarization flights took me to San Francisco – my first time crossing the Atlantic. I was immediately filled with the desire to explore: to see more of the world, experience new cities, and meet people from different backgrounds. “My job has led to lifelong bonds.” Marie Hecker Wearing the uniform for the first time was an equally meaningful moment for me. It immediately created a sense of belonging. With that uniform, I became part of a community in which people from all backgrounds come together and work as one. That wealth of perspectives and experiences continues to be a source of inspiration to this day. And although we are constantly assigned to new crews, some of those connections have grown into lifelong bonds. I have a “flying mom” who has become as close to me as a second mother. And I have a friend who is now my maid of honor. After 14 years in the profession – and a decade in my additional role as a First Class flight attendant – I am now primarily assigned to long-haul routes. At Lufthansa, anyone looking to advance their career in the cabin must complete the SMP, a service management qualification co-developed by Lufthansa and certified by the German Chamber of Industry and Commerce (IHK) as a professional qualification in service management. Today, the SMP is among the requirements for working in First Class or becoming a purser. First Class positions involve an additional selection process that candidates must apply for. Before joining the First Class team, we receive specialized training in refined service techniques, service etiquette, wine knowledge, and in-depth product expertise. What remains essential for every flight attendant, however, is the regular mandatory training. Every year, we practice emergency scenarios for each aircraft type we are assigned to operate on. These are the kinds of drills that help overcome the instinctive freeze response when a real emergency occurs. We also complete annual first aid training to ensure we are prepared to respond when it matters most. “Suddenly, you're many things at once: safety officer, firefighter, police officer, nurse, psychologist, childcare provider – and on top of that, a server.” Marie Hecker “I grew up with and alongside Lufthansa.” That sentence captures it perfectly, because this job has taught me so much. Once the aircraft door closes and is secured, I suddenly become many things at once: a safety officer, firefighter, police officer, nurse, psychologist, childcare provider, mediator – and on top of that, a server. When someone asks me whether they should apply, I always say the same thing: the only application you will ever regret is the one you never sent.
