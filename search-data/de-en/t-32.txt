At Cantina Do Mori, one of the city’s most traditional bàcari near Rialto, these Venetian snacks are served in the classic style with a glass of wine, known here as an ombra; a Crodino is a perfect non-alcoholic alternative, capturing the spirit of the aperitivo ritual without alcohol. Cicchetti usually consist of slices of bread (crostini) topped with polenta, seafood, meat, or vegetables, and are often enjoyed standing up. It is a classic in Venice and the surrounding Veneto region, and is often topped with Parmesan and butter. Baccalà mantecato: Baccalà mantecato is one of Venice’s best-known specialties: a finely blended cream of salted cod, usually served on bread or white polenta. It is particularly interesting to note that the fish does not traditionally come from the lagoon, but arrived in Venice as dried cod from Norway – a quiet reminder of just how far the city’s trade networks once stretched. Risotto al nero di seppia: Rice has always played an important role in Venetian cuisine, as the region is one of Italy’s major rice-growing areas. Accordingly, risotto al nero di seppia is also one of Venice’s best-known dishes: the squid ink dyes the rice a deep black and gives it its characteristic sea flavor. As is so often the case in Italy, there is no single definitive recipe here either. Sometimes tomato sauce is added, sometimes it is left out; some cook the risotto with garlic, while others deliberately avoid seasonings to allow the flavor of the sea to shine through. With St. Mark’s Basilica, the Doge’s Palace, the Campanile, the Museo Correr, the Archaeological Museum, and the Torre dell’Orologio, the city’s political, religious, and cultural history converges here within a compact space. The present stone structure by Antonio da Ponte was built at the end of the 16th century as a bold bridge spanning a single marble arch. With its rows of shops and sweeping views across the Grand Canal, the Rialto Bridge remains one of the city’s most impressive sights to this day. Especially in the evening hours, when the light reflects off the water, it exudes that special atmosphere for which Venice is famous. With its canals, small shops, and quieter alleyways, Cannaregio thus feels like a part of Venice where history is still immediately visible in the cityscape. Peggy Guggenheim Collection: The Peggy Guggenheim Collection is one of the most important museums of 20th-century art in Italy and is situated directly on the Grand Canal in the Palazzo Venier dei Leoni, the collector’s former residence. La Fenice can be experienced in various ways: while watching an opera or concert performance, on a self-guided tour with an audio guide, or as part of a guided tour. ["Discover the best things to do in Vilnius at a glance, with tips on food, sights, and experiences for an inspiring stay.","Legend has it that a Grand Duke founded the city after hearing an iron wolf howl in a dream. The Lithuanian capital, situated on the banks of the Neris and Vilnia rivers, has retained the mythical spirit of its founding to this day. Discover an Old Town with Baroque splendor, the wonderful laid-back atmosphere of the Užupis artists' quarter, and much more Šaltibarščiai (English: cold borscht), a cold beetroot soup, is Lithuania’s bright pink culinary signature. Made with kefir and freshly grated beetroot, it creates a tangy, refreshing dish that cools and satisfies on hot days without feeling heavy. It is served with dill, a hard-boiled egg, and warm boiled potatoes on the side. In Vilnius, šaltibarščiai can be found on almost every menu, in the Old Town as well as in the eateries around Stiklių gatvė. (© Shutterstock; header image © GoVilnius) More Culinary Delights: Cepelinai: These potato dumplings, named after zeppelins, are considered by many to be Lithuania’s unofficial national dish. Filled with minced meat or quark and topped with a bacon sauce, they represent the hearty side of Baltic cuisine. At the traditional restaurant Etno Dvaras near the cathedral, they are prepared according to an old recipe. Kibinai: These crescent-shaped pastries originate from the Karaites, a Turkic-speaking minority who have lived in Lithuania since the 14th century. Traditionally filled with lamb and onions, they are crispy, juicy, and a popular snack to be enjoyed among the stalls at the Halės Turgus market hall. Šakotis: This cake, which resembles a German Baumkuchen, is rotated over an open fire until it takes on a distinctive spiky shape. Soft on the inside and caramelized and crispy on the outside, it is a staple of festivals and holidays. In the patisseries around Pilies Boulevard, this delicate cake can also be found as a souvenir. Vilnius boasts one of the largest preserved Old Towns in Europe, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1994. A vibrant historic city center reveals itself amid Gothic church towers, Baroque façades, and hidden courtyards. Cobbled streets lead to small galleries, cafés, and quiet squares. Anyone entering the Old Town through the Gate of Dawn will pass one of the most significant Marian shrines in Eastern Europe. (© GoVilnius) Also Worth Seeing: Gediminas Tower: Perched on Castle Hill above the city stands the last remaining tower of the Upper Castle, the symbol of Vilnius for more than 500 years. From the viewing platform there is a panoramic view over the rooftops of the Old Town, river bends, and green hills. The walk up through the park is particularly worthwhile in the evening, when the light falls softly over the city. Republic of Užupis: In 1997, the neighborhood on the other side of the Vilnia playfully declared itself an independent republic, complete with its own constitution, which can be read on a wall in dozens of languages. Behind this lies a vibrant artists’ quarter with studios, street art, and a creative energy that remains palpable between the old buildings and lush greenery. St. Anne’s Church: This late-Gothic brick church is one of the most intricate buildings in the region. Its façade, made up of 33 different brick shapes, exhibits an almost textile-like pattern that appears different depending on the light. Napoleon is said to have admired it so much in 1812 that he would have loved to take it back to Paris. It is considered the masterpiece of Lithuanian Gothic architecture. From the water, Vilnius reveals yet another side to itself. A kayak or canoe trip on the Vilnia takes you through green gorges right in the heart of the city, past overgrown banks, and under old bridges. It is surprisingly quiet for a capital city. Various operators set off near Užupis and offer short tours suitable for beginners with no prior experience. It’s sightseeing and an urban nature experience all in one. (© GoVilnius) More Experiences: Explore Halės Turgus: Vilnius’ central market hall has been combining trade and indulgence under one roof since the 19th century. Street food stalls and small bars are distributed between the stalls selling smoked fish, Lithuanian cheese, and fresh bread. The atmosphere is liveliest on Saturday mornings, making it a great place to strike up a conversation with locals. Street art tour of Šnipiškės: This historic district north of the Neris, where many traditional timber buildings can still be seen, is changing rapidly and is now one of the city’s most exciting places. Large murals by international and Lithuanian artists cover firewalls and concrete walls. The neighborhood is easy to explore on foot at your own pace. The contrasts between Soviet architecture and contemporary art tell the city’s story without the need for information boards. Sunset at Three Crosses Hill: Three white crosses on a wooded hill east of the Old Town are one of the city’s most evocative landmarks, closely linked to Lithuanian identity and the history of freedom. The short, steep climb through the pine forest leads to a viewpoint that is particularly striking at sunset.
