["Discover seven people whose pioneering spirit, courage, and vision helped shape Lufthansa.","In our 100-year history, there have been many formative events and successes: spectacular long-distance flights in the early years, new aircraft types, technical innovations, and ever-increasing comfort on board. All of this has been made possible by people. Meet seven of them who paved the way and shaped Lufthansa into the iconic airline it is today When Deutsche Luft Hansa was continuously expanding its route network in Germany and Europe at the end of the 1920s, flying was still anything but routine. People navigated by eye, changes to the weather could alter flight schedules within minutes, and each new route entered unknown territory. During this pioneering age, Marga von Etzdorf wrote aviation history: in 1928, she was the first woman to be hired in the flying service of Deutsche Luft Hansa and initially flew as a co-pilot on domestic German routes. Even then, the young pilot stood out for her extraordinary ambition and great flying skills. Later, her spectacular long-distance flights – including as far as Tokyo – attracted attention. She became one of the best-known pilots of her time far beyond Germany and a symbol of the pioneering spirit of early aviation. Lufthansa was also in a phase of growth during these years. Founded in 1926, the company quickly developed into one of Europe's leading airlines. The route network was growing, international connections were being established, and new aircraft types were expanding the possibilities of scheduled services. Few other figures represent this courage to break down boundaries – geographically and socially – as much as Marga von Etzdorf. Marga von Etzdorf, the first woman to serve as a flight attendant for Deutsche Luft Hansa (© Lufthansa Bildarchiv; header image © Lufthansa Bildarchiv) While Lufthansa was expanding its route network, one man was providing the technical basis for it. As an engineer, entrepreneur, and founder of Junkers Luftverkehr, Hugo Junkers was one of the most influential figures in early aviation. In 1926, his airline merged with Deutsche Aero Lloyd to form the newly founded Deutsche Luft Hansa – and his aircraft also shaped the young airline from the very beginning. With the Junkers F 13, he developed the world's first all-metal commercial aircraft and set new standards in terms of safety, reliability, and cost-effectiveness. A few years later, the Ju 52 became the backbone of the fleet and shaped the image of Deutsche Luft Hansa for a long time. Junkers did not think of aviation as an exclusive adventure, but as a modern transport system. His technical innovations created the conditions for individual flight connections to become reliable scheduled services. They thus formed an important foundation for Lufthansa's rise to become an internationally networked airline. Hugo Junkers, whose aircraft once dominated the Lufthansa fleet (7th from the left; © Getty Images) At the end of the Second World War, Deutsche Luft Hansa ceased its flight operations; under Allied control, no air traffic was permitted at first. It was not until the early 1950s that a new chapter began. The Aktiengesellschaft für Luftverkehrsbedarf – LUFTAG for short – was founded in 1953 and took on the traditional name Lufthansa a year later. One of the architects of this new beginning was Hans M. Bongers, who became the first Chairman of the Executive Board of the new Deutsche Lufthansa AG. The task that awaited him was enormous: he had to recruit personnel, procure aircraft, negotiate international traffic rights – and ensure that German aviation was trusted again. On April 1, 1955, Lufthansa resumed scheduled services, followed shortly afterward by international connections and the first flights across the Atlantic in the same year. Within a short time, Lufthansa was an internationally operating airline once more. Hans M. Bongers, the architect behind Lufthansa's new beginning: (© Lufthansa Bildarchiv) At the same time as Lufthansa was restarting in the mid-1950s, travel as a whole was also changing. Flying was becoming more comfortable, the number of international connections was increasing, and with them passenger expectations were growing. The new airline wanted not only to carry its guests reliably to their destination, it also wanted to offer them comfort and make them feel welcome. Margot von Engelmann was one of the first stewardesses, as female flight attendants were still called at the time, of the new Lufthansa, which was relaunched in 1955, and witnessed this development from the very beginning. For example, she was on board the first transatlantic flight to New York on June 8, 1955 and accompanied many of the young airline's new routes. For many passengers, she became the face of a Lufthansa that stood for new beginnings, cosmopolitanism, and hospitality. Later, she often recalled how exclusive flying was back then: the crew members knew many of their guests personally. With the Lockheed Super Constellation and, from 1960, the Boeing 707, travel times got shorter. The world was moving closer together and Lufthansa was gradually developing into an international airline. Be it Bangkok or Rio de Janeiro, places that were previously considered exotic were suddenly easily accessible. Margot von Engelmann set standards that still shape the Lufthansa brand today: with charm and elegance, she made every flight an experience for guests – and thus shaped a service concept that is still closely associated with the Lufthansa brand to this day. Margot von Engelmann, flight attendant and symbol of Lufthansa's excellent service (© Deutsche Lufthansa AG/Bildarchiv) As Lufthansa became more and more international in the 1960s, expectations of its appearance also grew. Modern aircraft alone were no longer enough – an airline that connected people around the globe also needed an unmistakable image. In the early 1960s, Lufthansa commissioned renowned German graphic designer Otl Aicher with this task. Aicher carefully refined the crane, which designer Otto Firle had already created for Deutsche Luft-Reederei in 1918, and made it the central element of a corporate design that was considered down to the smallest detail. The clear typography, the characteristic Lufthansa blue, the reduced color scheme, and a consistently uniform brand identity set new standards. This new visual identity emerged during a time of growth. With modern jets, Lufthansa was continuously expanding its international route network, tapping into new markets, and establishing itself as one of Europe's leading airlines. Aicher's design gave this new era an unmistakable face. His corporate design is still considered a milestone today – and the crane is one of the most famous trademarks in the world. Otl Aicher, graphic designer and mastermind behind Lufthansa's modern brand identity (© Karsten de Riese/Bavarian State Library) In October 1977, Lufthansa captain Jürgen Schumann piloted flight LH181 from Palma de Mallorca to Frankfurt. The plane, later known worldwide by its name \"Landshut,\" was hijacked by terrorists and had to make numerous stopovers in various countries over several days. Schumann tried to protect passengers and crew under the most difficult conditions. When he talked to the Yemeni authorities in Aden about rescuing the hostages, he was shot dead by the kidnappers. The release of the hostages a few days later in Mogadishu, Somalia, marked a turning point – not only for Lufthansa, but for international aviation as a whole. Safety concepts were fundamentally revised, crisis management was professionalized, and the preparation of cockpit and cabin crews for exceptional situations was significantly intensified. To this day, Jürgen Schumann stands for a sense of responsibility, a sense of duty, and courage – qualities that cannot be laid down in manuals, but are demonstrated by people like Schumann. Jürgen Schumann, captain and tragic hero of the “Landshut” hijacking (© imago images) When Jürgen Weber took over as Chairman of the Executive Board of Lufthansa in 1991, the industry was once again faced with profound change. The liberalization of air transport, increasing competition, and advancing globalization were fundamentally changing business. A nationally oriented airline had to become an internationally competitive company. Weber had known Lufthansa for almost a quarter of a century at this point. The engineer joined the company in 1967 and helped shape the airline's transformation in various management positions. As Chairman of the Executive Board, he consistently pushed ahead with modernization: Lufthansa was privatized, invested in a modern fleet, established new business areas, and strategically aligned on an international level. A milestone in this development was the founding of Star Alliance in 1997 together with Air Canada, SAS, Thai Airways International, and United Airlines. The collaboration resulted in the largest aviation alliance in the world to date – and a model that changed international aviation in the long term. At the same time, under Weber, Lufthansa gradually evolved from a traditional airline into a global airline group with strong partnerships and a clear quality standard – which Lufthansa still exemplifies today.
