At first glance, not much. Yet Lufthansa, CDLP, and Jorinde Voigt share a vision of what makes a travel experience truly special: attention to detail, a commitment to quality, and the belief that true luxury goes beyond material things. Flying holds a particular fascination for her. “I always love seeing airplanes from below. You start wondering: Where are they going? What if I were sitting on that plane right now?” During a period when Voigt was traveling between two or three cities a week, the constant movement between places became a subject of her art. In “Intercontinental II,” she brings together different perspectives on flying: the view of the Earth from an airplane and, conversely, the view of an aircraft in the sky from the ground. Aircraft types, speeds, and compass directions serve as starting points for a drawing that does not document a specific flight route but instead depicts a range of possible movements. Now, in a sense, her art returns to the source of its inspiration – as a design on a garment that accompanies people on their travels. Transforming an artistic concept into a garment that meets the highest standards of aesthetics and comfort was CDLP’s task. Andreas Palm, co-founder and CEO of CDLP, explains why the Swedish brand and Lufthansa are such a natural fit: “An aircraft cabin and a garment are both the result of hundreds of small decisions that most people never consciously notice. When developing the loungewear, the feel of the fabric against the skin was just as important as the design itself. The ensemble is made from TENCEL™ Lyocell, an exceptionally soft, breathable fiber. “The material has the softness and drape of silk but is far more practical for the conditions on board,” Palm explains. It helps regulate body temperature and is relatively resistant to wrinkles. The fiber is derived from responsibly managed wood sources. For CDLP, the conscious selection of materials is an integral part of its understanding of luxury. For Voigt, the fabric also has a poetic dimension. ["Artist Jorinde Voigt speaks about her fascination with flying, inspiring travel destinations, and her collaboration with Lufthansa.","Jorinde Voigt is one of Germany’s most important contemporary artists – and she’s fascinated by flying. Here, she talks about how she translates this fascination into her art, how she likes to pass the time on an aircraft, and what makes her collaboration with Lufthansa so special Ms. Voigt, which cities do you travel to again and again? Milan, Frankfurt, New York, Houston, Paris, and Palermo. Which of these is your favorite? My favorite city is Milan. What fascinates me there above all are the building entrances. They are all different, yet all very elaborate and designed with an enormous level of craftsmanship. I don’t know of anything like it in any other city. Which destination did you have no particular expectations for, but were positively surprised by? Sydney. I ended up there completely spontaneously. I wasn’t prepared for it, and then I was really blown away. The city turned out to be incredibly inspiring. I had three big ideas, which I worked on over the next three years. The work that Lufthansa and I have now chosen for our joint project was also created during that time. Portrait of artist Jorinde Voigt sitting in profile on a dark wooden woven chair against a minimalist studio wall. You mention the exclusive loungewear for Lufthansa First Class. Your work “Intercontinental II” is printed on both the top and the pants. Why does this particular drawing suit the project so well? Because it addresses not only the technical construction of aircraft and their different types, but also space: with the intersecting airspace and the Earth’s surface. It describes the process of travel: losing contact with the ground and finding it again somewhere else. Can you explain that in more detail? “Intercontinental II” is not about depicting specific flight recordings or flight paths. The aircraft types serve as parameters and at the same time give the work a real point of reference, because they are aircraft I have flown on myself. I am interested in the interplay of two perspectives: sitting in an aircraft and imagining the place below you, or seeing an aircraft from the ground and imagining that you are sitting in it and leaving the place where you are. This creates a space of possibilities. The age-old human dream of flying also resonates here, awakened by the sight of an aircraft. How did flying become the subject of your art? My art is always inspired directly by my life. When “Intercontinental II” was created, I was traveling a lot, often two or three cities or countries a week. This constant flying and the change between places became the subject. However, I had already been exploring airspace and the question of how time and distance can be represented through the flight paths of eagles or of different aircraft types, for example. Your fascination with flying goes so far that you even began training as a pilot. What impressed you most the first time you sat in the cockpit? First of all, it was gigantic. For me, the most interesting experience was that, as a pilot, you orient yourself to the horizon. What do you always have with you when you travel? Drawing paper, a pencil, a fineliner, and a book. Is that also how you like to pass the time on a flight? Drawing? No, I prefer lying back, lost in my imagination. Unless it’s daylight and I can watch the sky, because then I don’t want to miss a minute. I find that absolutely fantastic and incomparably beautiful. What do you find especially exciting about flying? There are two things. I find it fascinating to fly over cities at night and see those glowing patterns. You realize that we are basically an organism too. You get a sense of the collective and of the forms we create on Earth. I also love observing different weather conditions from the aircraft. It’s raining at the back right, and at the front it’s completely different. I even like turbulence: when it gets a little bumpy, I think that’s really great. You were a master-class student of the artist Katharina Sieverding, who, unlike you, became famous mainly for photography. What did you take from that time for your own work? Katharina Sieverding was teaching at the Berlin University of the Arts back then. It was a hotspot for a truly critical engagement with the present. Since my background was in sociology, philosophy, and literature, I thought it was great that there was someone in the visual arts who was interested in precise thinking and had the political ambition to engage with reality as it is. She did this with photographic means, and I do it with drawing. Why did you choose a different path? At the time, I felt that photography was the most powerful medium in terms of social and political impact. That’s why I wanted to really get to know it. However, I reached my personal limits because I longed to include multiple perspectives in my work, to look at subjects from different angles. I only managed to achieve this through a radical reduction of means. Today I really only need pencil and paper. Do you try to put the world in order with your art, or do you rather want to highlight its complexity? I think we look at the world in order to understand our inner selves. We always walk around with puzzles inside us that we try to solve. The outer world and the inner world are in constant dialogue. It’s about developing an approach that weaves the many dimensions of outside and inside together with time and space. Jorinde Voigt was born in 1977 in Frankfurt am Main. She lives and works in Berlin and has also been a Professor of Painting and Drawing at the Hamburg University of Fine Arts since 2019. In her drawings, paintings, and sculptural works, she explores, among other things, perception, memory, emotion, and questions from the natural sciences and philosophy. ["Two sleep experts explain how to sleep on a plane, with advice on seats, neck support, noise, light, power naps, and caffeine.","Sleeping on a plane sounds like the perfect way to make a long flight fly by. But your body doesn’t always cooperate. We spoke to two sleep experts to find out what really helps you to arrive feeling rested – and what doesn’t A window seat puts you in control of the shade, and you won’t be disturbed when your seatmates get up. Sitting in front of the wings also means less noise, because engine noise tends to travel toward the rear of the aircraft. One more tip: exit row seats offer extra legroom, but there are eligibility requirements. Children, for example, cannot sit in these rows. When you lie down, your muscles can relax. When you sit upright, however, the muscles supporting your head, neck, and upper body have to keep working. This becomes especially tricky during REM sleep, the stage when most dreaming occurs. The body temporarily switches off nearly all musculoskeletal activity, a state known as muscle atonia, to keep us from acting out our dreams. As your muscles relax, your head may drop forward, jolting you awake and forcing your body to correct its position. On a long-haul flight, these repeated interruptions can fragment your sleep. A supportive neck pillow helps, especially if you can lean against the wall by the window. An added bonus: you’re less likely to snore when sitting upright. on overnight long-haul flights lasting ten hours or more, we offer sleep masks to guests in every travel class? They can help you settle into sleep mode. Unlike your eyes, your ears can’t simply close. “Our auditory system stays connected to our surroundings even when we’re asleep. That’s an evolutionary holdover. People sleeping outdoors in the past had to be able to react to predators and other dangers at any moment,” explains Dr. Hans-Günter Weeß, a sleep specialist and psychotherapist. That built-in early warning system keeps running on a plane, causing us to stir at every clatter. Noise-canceling headphones or earplugs can help. And because light is the strongest cue for our sleep-wake cycle, a sleep mask is another must-have. Airplane cabins are rarely completely quiet. Small aids can make a big difference when it comes to relaxation (© Felix Wittich) You don’t have to sleep to arrive feeling refreshed. Dr. Michael Feld, a sleep medicine specialist based in Cologne, recommends Jacobson’s progressive muscle relaxation: briefly tense individual muscle groups, notice the tension, then release it. Paired with slow, calming breathing exercises, this technique can help your body unwind, even while sitting upright. “Sleep comes from relaxing and letting go. If you want to unwind while sitting, consciously relax your body – for example, through progressive muscle relaxation.” Dr. Michael Feld How to Relax on a Plane While seated, lift your heels to tense your calves. Hold for ten seconds, then release. Spend twenty seconds focusing on the feeling of relaxation in your calves. Tense your thigh muscles firmly for ten seconds, then release. Focus on the relaxed feeling in your thighs for twenty seconds. Tense your upper arms (biceps) for ten seconds, then release. Focus on relaxing those muscles for twenty seconds. Raise your shoulders toward your ears, then release. Massage your face for ten seconds, followed by twenty seconds of relaxation. On medium-haul flights, one or two power naps may be your best option. The key is keeping them short: after about thirty minutes, the body begins to enter deep sleep, and waking up during this phase can leave you feeling groggy. This is known as sleep inertia. Keep your naps shorter, and you’re more likely to wake up refreshed rather than disoriented. “The rule of thumb is to keep naps short and controlled, rather than sleeping until you’re completely exhausted,” says Dr. Hans-Günter Weeß. On long-haul flights, restful sleep is especially valuable, and the right preparation can help you get it (© Jens Goerlich) Caffeine is a stimulant and, depending on how much you consume and how sensitive you are to it, its effects can last for up to eleven hours. If you’re hoping to sleep on the plane, it’s best to avoid coffee and other caffeinated drinks. our Sleeper’s Row now offers Economy Class guests even more comfort on long-haul flights? With an entire row of three seats to yourself, you can stretch out, relax, and sleep. A mattress topper, blanket, and Business Class-quality pillow make the journey even more restful. Availability is limited, and Sleeper’s Row can be booked shortly before departure at check-in or at the gate. “On flights from east to west, however, caffeine can help build up sleep pressure for nighttime at your destination. That can support your body in adjusting more quickly to the new sleep-wake schedule.” Dr. Hans-Günter Weeß If you’re having trouble falling asleep on a plane, soothing podcasts or guided meditations may help you drift off. And even if you stay awake, being relaxed can make the flight much more comfortable. In a state of relaxed wakefulness, the brain produces alpha waves. The alpha rhythm refers to brainwave frequencies of roughly eight to twelve hertz, typically observed when we’re awake but relaxed. “The alpha rhythm falls between active wakefulness, associated with beta waves, and the onset of sleep, associated with theta waves. It is considered a state of deep mental rest, even though you’re technically still awake.” Dr. Michael Feld Dr. Michael Feld is a physician specializing in general medicine and sleep medicine, and a certified sleep specialist with the German Society for Sleep Research and Sleep Medicine (DGSM).
