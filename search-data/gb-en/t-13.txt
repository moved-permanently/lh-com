Exiting row by row speeds up the process of leaving the aircraft Unwritten rule: the person in the middle seat gets both armrests When composure suddenly becomes stylish The most relaxed message in the collection: Boarding by Group Hits Different. Whoever wears it sends a quiet signal to the world — that they figured out boarding long ago. It isn't a race. There are no medals for the first person at the gate. The seat with the best deal Now to the great question of fairness in civil aviation, that quiet battle waged in every row of three: the armrest. Middle Seat gets the Armrest gets straight to the point and ends the debate before it even begins. The logic behind it is compelling. The person at the window gets to lean back and dream out the porthole. The person on the aisle stretches their legs and reaches the drinks cart first. And whoever sits in the middle gets both armrests in return — fair deal, right? Anyone wearing this wisdom on their sleeve may not even need to say it out loud. A glance down is enough, and the matter is settled. Responsibility, a few inches back Recline Responsibly is perhaps the most diplomatic message in the whole collection, because here, as we know, two hearts beat in one chest. Of course you're allowed to recline your seat (that's what the button is for, after all). The only question is whether those few inches of tilt on a 30-minute flight are worth half a cup of coffee for the row behind. The solution is disarmingly simple, and it costs nothing: a quick glance back, a friendly \"Is this okay for you?\" and a potential conflict turns into a small gesture of consideration. Reclining responsibly doesn't mean not reclining at all. It just means knowing, for a moment, who's sitting behind you. Row by row to the exit The end of every flight is the same the world over: the moment the plane stops, the whole cabin springs up as one and then stands there, motionless, because naturally nothing is moving. Live, Love, Exit by Row is the affectionate reminder that getting off works remarkably well when it happens one row at a time. It's one of those truths everyone knows and yet no one ever follows. And yet leaving the aircraft goes noticeably faster when you let the row in front of you go first, rather than jumping into starting position too soon. Von Hoyos would probably smile gently here, because as he says: \"The supreme discipline of politeness is looking past the misbehavior of others.\" Sometimes, though, a well-placed line on a cap does the trick too. And for everyone else, the same holds true: It's okay not to Clap when the Plane Lands. Both are perfectly fine. You hear the applause less often than you used to, perhaps because flying has become so ordinary for many. Whoever wants to clap should feel free, ideally in rhythm and with plenty of feeling, as a spontaneous thank-you to a crew that has just finished a long shift and brought the aircraft safely back to earth.
