["inside the Starlink installation on an Airbus A320neo: how Lufthansa Group Wi-Fi will bring high-speed internet on board.","The Lufthansa Group will be equipping its entire fleet with high-speed internet from Starlink by 2029. The system has been installed for the first time at Lufthansa’s Technical Fleet Management maintenance center at Berlin Brandenburg Airport (BER) in a Lufthansa Airbus A320neo. We visited the site The Airbus A320neo “Freising” stands in the Lufthansa maintenance hangar at BER Airport with scaffolding around its nose and tail. Mechanics in yellow Lufthansa safety vests hurry through the space. A female technician is working on one of the engines – thanks to sophisticated gear technology, these are among the most advanced turbines in modern aviation. Muffled voices and the sounds of work being carried out drift from within the aircraft. Outside, rain is pounding on the tarmac, and the rumble of thunder echoes around. Inside the hangar, there’s little sign of the bad weather. Eight aircraft can be serviced simultaneously at the Berlin facility. Around 220 technicians and engineers working in three shifts, along with 40 administrative staff from planning, engineering, and product management, work to keep the aircrafts’ downtime as short as possible. The “Freising” has been in the hangar for two weeks; it’s undergoing what’s known as a C-Check, a scheduled inspection – and so far, everything is going according to plan. Just Business As Usual Then? Quite the contrary. For the on-site staff, Lufthansa Airlines, and the Lufthansa Group as a whole, this stormy early summer day marks a first. The “Freising” is the first aircraft in the Lufthansa fleet to be equipped with high-performance internet access from Starlink – visible to passengers as Lufthansa Group Wi-Fi. The system ensures reliable and secure internet access on board at all times, with only minimal latency. On the aircraft, the antenna – which measures about half a square meter – is fitted into the mount and attached directly to the fuselage (© Oscar Bravo; header image © Johannes Mink/Lufthansa) “With Starlink, we can offer our passengers high-speed internet from gate to gate for the first time,” says Christian Radius. The aerospace engineer is responsible for the technical execution of the Starlink installation at the BER site and serves as the point of contact for the production team in the case of any implementation questions. After the “Freising,” plans are in place to equip another 15 Airbus A320neo aircraft here. Lufthansa Technik’s heavy maintenance facilities in Budapest and Sofia are also being utilized at the same time. By 2029, the entire Lufthansa Group fleet – approximately 850 aircraft – is expected to be equipped with the Starlink system. This would make it the largest European airline to use this technology. Installing the Wi-Fi system is a complex process that requires collaboration between specialists in the structural engineering field – who are responsible for the aircraft fuselage – and electronics engineers. “To ensure high-performance connectivity at every seat, we’re installing two Starlink systems in every A320neo,” explains Radius. “Four routers in the cabin process the signals from two antennas mounted on the fuselage – one at the front of the cabin and one at the rear.” Installing the Wi-Fi system is complex and requires collaboration among specialists (© Oscar Bravo) High-speed internet at the touch of a button, right from the cockpit (© Oscar Bravo) Ceiling panels in the cabin are removed to allow for the installation. The scheduled C-Check maintenance and the associated removal of the seat rows provide an opportunity for knowledge transfer and sharing experiences between other colleagues in the Lufthansa Group and Lufthansa Technik employees, as they allow the colleagues to get a close-up look at the system and how it is integrated into the cabin. They can see what is usually hidden behind the plastic panels: a colorful bundle of cables running through the aircraft. The cable for satellite internet is thin and white and now has to be connected to the antennas through an opening in the cabin shell. To do this, a technician – securely roped up – positions himself at the front of the fuselage, with one of the flat, tile-shaped antennas, measuring about half a square meter in size, in front of him. With a few practiced movements, the cable is plugged in, the antenna is fitted into the bracket, and the bracket, along with the antenna, is mounted on the fuselage. During flight, the system will automatically connect to the nearest Starlink satellite. By 2029, all approximately 850 aircraft across the Lufthansa Group fleet will be equipped with Starlink technology (© Jens Görlich/Lufthansa) “What sets Starlink apart from all other providers is its almost seamless coverage,” says Tim Kaya, who, as Group Senior Fleet Manager for Cabin Interiors, is responsible for integrating the system into the cabin Wi-Fi. “Starlink currently has more than 10,000 satellites in orbit; the next closest competitor has about 650.” For an increasing number of passengers (and, from 2029, for all passengers) flying with the Lufthansa Group, this means stable internet access throughout the entire flight – be it to work, communicate with family, friends, or colleagues, or consume media content. “With Lufthansa Group Wi-Fi, they’ll experience a whole new way of traveling,” Kaya promises. Using the service on board is free – and incredibly easy. Travelers simply need a Lufthansa Travel ID or their Miles & More service card number. You can register for the Travel ID in just a few steps on the Lufthansa website using your email address and a few details. Once on the plane, passengers just need to log in to the online portal and they’ll be connected to the internet via Wi-Fi. It’s important to remember that, as before, airplane mode must be enabled during the flight. There are also a few rules to keep in mind when using Lufthansa Group Wi-Fi above the clouds: Wear headphones: to ensure a pleasant flight for all passengers, headphones are required when listening to music, watching movies, or using entertainment apps. No phone calls: for the same reason, phone calls and video calls are not permitted. No live streaming: live video and audio streaming from the aircraft – such as for social media channels – is prohibited. However, recording content for later publication is permitted. The first official flight of the A320neo “Freising” equipped with Starlink high-speed internet took off on August 19. It headed south – above the clouds, but connected to the world. ["Tino Klähne from the Lufthansa Innovation Hub talks about smart cabins, in-flight internet, seamless travel, biometrics, and Cabinblu.","How will we travel in the future? We asked Tino Klähne from the Lufthansa Innovation Hub this question. Our conversation covers smart cabins, seamless travel, and realistic visions of the future Lufthansa Insights: Mr. Klähne, when you look into the future of flying – are we talking about a radical upheaval or a series of small steps? Tino Klähne: We're definitely talking about an evolution, not a revolution. In 15 or 25 years, an aircraft will still look the same as it does today: fuselage, wings, cabin, engines. The big leaps – like Concorde back in the day – are extremely difficult to implement in terms of financing and regulations. What is changing massively are the digital services and experiences around the flight. What do you think are the main drivers of change? Two things: the aircraft cabin and connectivity. As an airline, we are heavily reliant on what new aircraft Airbus, Boeing, and other manufacturers deliver. At the same time, we are seeing a trend toward retrofitting, i.e. modernizing existing cabins more quickly with new seats, better power supply, holders for tablets, and more charging points. The real game changer, however, is stable, fast internet on board. With Starlink, for example, Wi-Fi will be available in the Lufthansa Group in the future and the quality will be the same as what you're used to at home – and it will be free of charge for guests. When it comes to the future of flying, the term smart cabin is often used. What does this mean for me as a passenger? The smart cabin is basically a networked cabin that forms a platform, which means that your own devices can be easily connected to the system, for example. Content and services are personalized, from entertainment to food and seating preferences. In addition, relevant travel information is communicated proactively to the passenger: \"Your connecting flight is secured, gate XY, boarding postponed by 20 minutes\" – directly on the screen or in the app, even before landing. For the crew, there is more contextual information in the background: who is afraid of flying? Who is traveling with three small children? Who needs help with connecting flights? Ideally, the cabin feels a bit like a Tesla at 39,000 feet: digital features can be added via software updates without having to reconfigure half the aircraft. Personalization quickly starts to sound like annoying advertising. Will the airplane become a flying billboard? The topic of retail media is indeed big in the industry – in other words, personalized advertising in in-flight entertainment. Technically, many things are possible, including AI-generated ads in real time. But the balance is crucial: we can also use the same data to improve services – for example by showing the crew who has special needs, who we should prioritize assisting. The goal is not to commercialize the cabin, but to make traveling even more enjoyable. To what extent will the entire journey – from getting out the taxi to reaching the gate – be digitally interlinked? Much more so than today – the goal must be for it all to be seamless. Some airports are already measuring waiting times at security and check-in and are starting to share this data. In the future, travelers could receive early messages such as: \"If you get out of the taxi in 18 minutes, you can get through security and boarding in a relaxed manner.\" In addition, there could be links with taxis, ridesharing services, and hotels. If a flight is delayed, ideally your driver already knows that. And in the medium term, biometrics will simplify the journey through the airport, including duty free shopping. The key technology here is \"Pay by face,\" which means you don't need money or a card, but instead your face is scanned and the amount debited from your account. Lufthansa has also been testing various biometric solutions for several years. Technology is one thing. What do you personally want in the future as a traveler? Above all, fewer surprises. I no longer want to have to click through parking information, luggage rules, seating plans, and special processes, but to be able to trust that solutions will be proposed at an early stage in the event of unexpected situations. Internally, we like to call this \"certainty of success\": a high degree of trust that the trip will happen without me having to control everything myself. What is the Lufthansa Innovation Hub doing specifically to make the flying experience even better for travelers? We are already helping people with a fear of flying through the Cabinblu app, which we launched in 2025. About 40 percent of passengers are afraid of flying and fly anyway. For this reason, we have developed digital support with the help of a leading psychologist – with explanations, exercises, and meditations. In the future, something like this could also be integrated into Lufthansa apps and systems, including a notification to the crew or the cockpit so they can react more sensitively. And what do you currently have in the pipeline? We are working on several projects, often together with teams from the Lufthansa Group. Most of them concern internal improvements. As far as travelers are concerned, we are currently digitalizing processes for people with special needs – from wheelchair users to families with small children, among other things. One aim is to coordinate special service requests such as wheelchair assistance more easily. Our goal is for there to be fewer exceptions, greater accessibility as standard, and less stress for everyone involved. Another field is AI for ground operations. At the moment, gate agents have to perform many complex steps in parallel, whereas we are working on solutions to automate routine tasks so that our gate employees have more time for real guest interaction. What about the really big leaps – hypersonics, air taxis, beaming? I'm afraid neither of us will live to see the beaming (laughs). We have evaluated topics such as supersonic or hypersonic aircraft, but in order to operate them economically, we would need to charge so much for tickets that most people would say: \"That's too much money just to save two hours.\" Air taxis will play a role in niche areas, but because of security, infrastructure, and connections, they will be less of a solution to the basic problem of overall travel time than some hope. All of this means that the future of flying will not be spectacularly reinvented overnight.
