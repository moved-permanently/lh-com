["Discover the best things to do in Bogotá at a glance, with tips on food, sights, and experiences for an inspiring stay.","Bogotá is located where you would hardly expect a metropolis with millions of inhabitants: at an altitude of over 2,600 meters (8,660 feet), nestled in the eastern Andes Cordillera. Bogotá is raw and refined, colonial heritage and vibrant present all in one. From golden myths to colonial alleyways to Sunday streets filled with life – discover here what Bogotá has to offer Ajiaco is the soul of Bogotá in a bowl: a creamy, powerful soup made from three different types of potatoes, tender chicken, and the aromatic herb guascas, which originally comes from the Colombian high Andes. Served with fresh cream and capers, this dish has warmed the inhabitants of the cool Sabana plateau for centuries. The traditional restaurant La Puerta Falsa in La Candelaria, which has been in operation since 1816, is considered the top spot for the culinary classic. (© Getty Images; header image © Getty Images) More Culinary Delights: Changua: Changua is Bogotá's unofficial breakfast: a simple but filling soup made with milk, water, eggs, and green onions, served with soaked bread. The dish dates back to colonial times and can be found in traditional tiendas and small breakfast cafés in the city center. Tamales bogotanos: Tamal bogotano is more than a dish – it's a Sunday ritual: cornmeal, vegetables, and hard-boiled eggs are wrapped in banana leaves and steamed for hours, often with chicken or pork. Early in the morning at the colorful Mercado de Paloquemao, one of the city's most vibrant markets, tamal bogotano is one of the most popular snacks. Chocolate santafereño: Chocolate santafereño is a traditional afternoon ritual from the colonial era: thick cocoa in which fresh cheese is dipped or melted, accompanied by soft almojábanas (buns) or pandeyuca (bread). The name refers to Santa Fé de Bogotá, the original colonial name of the city. In the old cafés of La Candelaria, this tradition lives on to this day. The Museo del Oro is one of the most fascinating museums in Latin America and houses one of the biggest gold collections in the world: countless pre-Colombian objects tell of a civilization that developed an advanced goldsmithing trade centuries before the Spanish Conquista. The centerpiece is the legendary Balsa Muisca, a tiny gold raft that is considered the immediate origin of the El Dorado myth. (© Museo del Oro Bogotá - Banrepcultural) Also Worth Seeing: Cerro de Monserrate: High above Bogotá's sea of houses towers the Cerro de Monserrate at 3,152 meters (10,341 feet), a place of pilgrimage with a white colonial church, which can be reached by cable car or funicular. The panoramic view of the endless metropolis is breathtaking in clear weather, especially early in the morning when wafts of fog are still drifting over the rooftops. La Candelaria: This historic district is the colonial heart of the city: narrow, brightly painted streets, venerable colonial buildings, and lively squares alternate with street markets and large graffiti murals. At the Plaza de Bolívar, the central square of La Candelaria, the history of Colombia can be experienced up close. Museo Botero: The Museo Botero doesn't just display works by the Colombian painter and sculptor Fernando Botero, it also has a significant collection of international masters such as Picasso, Dalí, and Monet. The tranquil atmosphere of the historic colonial villa makes it one of the most beautiful cultural spaces in the city. Every Sunday, the Ciclovía transforms Bogotá into a lively open-air stage: over 120 kilometers (75 miles) of urban main roads are closed to car traffic and opened to cyclists, runners, skaters, and walkers of all generations. What began as a small experiment in 1974 is now a weekly cultural ritual in which more than a million people regularly take part – and which cities all over the world have copied. (© Mauritius Alamy) More Experiences: Graffiti tours: Bogotá is considered a street art metropolis. Since the city officially recognized graffiti as an art form, large-scale murals by international and local artists have been created, making guided tours of neighborhoods such as La Candelaria, San Felipe, or Chapinero unforgettable insights into contemporary Colombian culture. Play tejo: Tejo is Colombia's noisy national sport: players throw heavy metal discs at a clay target filled with small packets of gunpowder. If you are looking for an authentic experience among locals, this is the place for you. Mercado de las Pulgas de Usaquén: Every weekend, the elegant Usaquén district comes alive with its winding streets and cafés: the Mercado de las Pulgas attracts visitors with antiques, handmade crafts, and Colombian street food cuisine in a relaxed atmosphere between colonial buildings and the shade of the trees. The \"Green Capital of Europe\" of 2016 captivates visitors with its car-free old town, the striking bridges designed by Jože Plečnik, and the imposing castle A piece of Slovenian food culture can be found in Ljubljana in the smallest spaces: the Kranjska klobasa (Carniolan sausage). The smoked pork sausage has been produced since the early 19th century according to a now protected original recipe – with garlic, pepper, and a minimum quality of meat that is required by law. This makes it more aromatic and less robustly seasoned than comparable Central European sausages. Snack bar Klobasarna near the Central Market is a prime example of this self-confident simplicity: a dish served with mustard, horseradish, and fresh white bread. © Getty Images More Culinary Delights: Potica: This rolled yeast dough specialty with fillings of nuts, tarragon, or poppy seeds is one of the country's culinary classics and is served mostly on special occasions. Craft coffee in Ljubljana: In roasteries such as Črno Zrno, coffee is brewed with a focus on origin and craftsmanship. In the wine bars of the old town, young winemakers rely on spontaneous fermentation and minimal intervention to create Slovenian natural wines that are attracting international attention. Žlikrofi: Filled with potato, onion, and herbs, these cushion-shaped dumplings from Idrija are typically served with brown butter – EU-protected since 2010 and unmistakably Slovenian. Ljubljana Castle (\"Ljubljanski grad\") towers high above the old town and offers a wide-ranging view over red roofs toward the Alps. At the same time, it is more than a traditional landmark, but a place where history, exhibitions, and cultural events intertwine. In the light of the early morning sun, the panorama from up here seems almost mystical. © Getty Images Also Worth Seeing: Tromostovje: This iconic triple bridge connects the old town and the modern center and exemplifies the signature of architect Jože Plečnik. Dragon Bridge: The copper dragon figures are considered a symbol of the city and combine Art Nouveau architecture with local mythology. Prešeren Square: The city's central square is a meeting place, stage, and postcard motif all at once. The pink Franciscan church dominates the picture, flanked by Art Nouveau buildings and the monument to national poet France Prešeren. A walk through Metelkova shows a completely different side of Ljubljana. The former barracks area has developed into a creative open space. Between the bars, studios, and small stages, a lively, varied creative scene has established itself. Our tip: come in the evening and admire the art over a drink. © Mankica Kranjec Photography / Visit Ljubljana More Experiences: Kayak tour on the Ljubljanica: From the water, the old town shows itself from a new perspective, while bridges and façades slowly pass by. Tivoli Park: The spacious and well-maintained green space with small wooded areas and open meadows borders the city center in the east and stretches to Roznik Hill in the west. Sunset on the Nebotičnik: The terrace of the 1930s skyscraper, once the tallest building in the Balkans, offers an expansive view over the rooftops of the city. Ready to discover Ljubljana? ["Experience Kuala Lumpur through the Petronas Towers, Batu Caves, nasi lemak, Jalan Alor, Chinatown, and KL Forest Eco Park.","Modern high-rise towers alongside magnificent colonial buildings, colorful temples dedicated to Hindu deities side by side with imposing mosques, traditional food stalls in Chinatown, and designer hotels in the city center – in Malaysia’s capital, Kuala Lumpur, the diversity of cultures shapes the cityscape and everyday life. Here are our tips for an inspiring stay in Kuala Lumpur Few other dishes are as symbolic of Malaysia as nasi lemak. Fragrant coconut rice is served with spicy sambal paste, roasted peanuts, anchovies, cucumber, and egg. The combination of sweetness, heat, and umami once made this dish the country’s most popular breakfast. Nowadays, the dish is served at any time of day. The modern restaurant Nalé at Pavilion Damansara Heights serves nasi lemak in several variations – with beef, lamb, salmon, and squid. (© Getty Images; header image © Getty Images) More Culinary Delights: Char Kway Teow: These rice noodles, stir-fried over an open flame with shrimp, egg, and soy sauce, are a legacy of Chinese immigrants but are now among the classics of Malaysian street food. They taste especially authentic at the food stalls along Jalan Alor, Kuala Lumpur’s bustling street food market. Banana Leaf Rice: This dish, which originated in South India, is – as the name suggests – served on a banana leaf. Rice, vegetable curries, and pickles (vegetables or fruit preserved in oil, spices, and salt), as well as crispy fried lentil wafers (papadums), are traditionally eaten with the hands. The Brickfields neighborhood, also known as Kuala Lumpur’s Little India, is considered the best place to experience this. Hokkien Mee: Thick wheat noodles are braised in dark soy sauce with pork, cabbage, and shrimp. This hearty dish is one of the culinary hallmarks of Kuala Lumpur’s Chinese community. Standing 452 meters (1,483 feet) tall, the two interconnected Petronas Towers, which were the world’s tallest buildings until 2004, dominate Kuala Lumpur’s skyline. The architecture, inspired by Islamic geometry, and the Islamic patterns on the façade blend with motifs inside the twin towers that are reminiscent of Malaysian handicrafts and weaving patterns. The silhouette of the twin towers is particularly impressive after dark, when the façade begins to glow. (© Getty Images) Also Worth Seeing: Batu Caves: About 15 kilometers (9 miles) north of the city center, 272 colorfully painted steps lead to one of Southeast Asia’s most important Hindu shrines. The massive limestone cave is guarded by a monumental golden statue dedicated to the god Murugan. Believers flock to the Batu Caves in droves, especially during the Thaipusam festival at the beginning of the year. Merdeka Square: It was on this square that Malaysia’s independence from British colonial rule was proclaimed in 1957. It is therefore not without a certain historical irony that some of the city’s most beautiful colonial buildings stand around the square, including the Sultan Abdul Samad Building, an impressive structure featuring Moorish, Mughal, and Victorian elements. Thean Hou Temple: Perched on a hill, this Chinese temple is one of the largest of its kind in Southeast Asia. Red lacquered columns, curved roofs, and the panoramic view of the Kuala Lumpur skyline create a special atmosphere. After sunset, the city reveals its contrasting facets. Amid the illuminated towers of the financial district, the street stalls of Bukit Bintang, and the rooftop bars offering views of the city at night, the nightlife comes alive in an urban setting that makes Kuala Lumpur’s energy palpable. Many places are easily accessible on foot. The city center (KLCC) in particular, as well as the Bukit Bintang and Bangsar neighborhoods, are very popular after dark and are considered safe for travelers. (© Getty Images) More Experiences: Explore Jalan Alor: The city’s most famous area for street food transforms into a kind of open-air restaurant in the evening. Vegetables and rice noodles sizzling in steaming woks, grilled satay skewers, and stalls selling tropical fruits create a lively, authentic atmosphere. Stroll through Chinatown: Around Petaling Street, traditional shops, Taoist and Hindu temples, and modern cafés and boutiques form the heart of Chinese culture in Kuala Lumpur. This is also where the alley once known as Drury Lane (now Jalan Sang Guna) is located; following extensive renovation, its colorful murals have made it a centerpiece of Kuala Lumpur’s creative scene. Visit KL Forest Eco Park: Right in the heart of the city, a treetop trail winds through one of the city’s last remaining patches of rainforest. The façades of skyscrapers keep peeking through the greenery of the trees, adding an exciting element to the walk across suspension bridges and boardwalks. Ready to discover Kuala Lumpur? Gleaming skyscrapers, colorful temples, bustling street food stalls – Malaysia’s capital never fails to impress. Ready to experience it for yourself?
