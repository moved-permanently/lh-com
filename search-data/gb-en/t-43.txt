["Red Hong Yi shares her creative neighborhood Kampung Attap in Kuala Lumpur, from art and cafés to galleries, restaurants, and local hot spots.","Red Hong Yi is one of Malaysia’s best-known artists and creates art from everyday objects – most recently, buttons. In the video, she shows us where she likes to shop for them and takes us to some of her favorite restaurants, cafés, and galleries in Kampung Attap, Kuala Lumpur’s creative hub Concrete, glass, and steel were once Red Hong Yi’s materials of choice. Today, she works with buttons, coins, eggshells, chopsticks, tea bags, soil, salt, socks, and matchsticks. Many of her works are made from vast quantities of small objects or everyday materials, earning Red the nickname “the artist who paints without a paintbrush.” Born in Malaysia, Red originally studied architecture in Melbourne. After completing her master’s degree, she joined Australian architecture firm Hassell in Shanghai, designing skyscrapers across Asia for the region’s then-booming construction industry. “When I designed a high-rise, I never knew whether it would actually get built,” Red says. “And even if it did, it would take at least six or seven years. I kept thinking, wow, I don’t even know if I’ll still be at the same company by then – or even in the same country.” So she began creating smaller art projects instead. “Something I could finish in a month,” Red says. She posted images of her work on social media and soon began receiving requests for commissioned pieces. “Some of the fees were pretty attractive,” she says. “So I decided to take the leap and become a full-time artist.” Her boss at the time made the decision easier. “He encouraged me and assured me that I could come back to the office if it didn’t work out. I had nothing to lose.” Today, Red lives and works in Kuala Lumpur – far from the world’s major international art capitals, but very much by choice. Malaysia, she says, is and always will be home. Her studio is in the central neighborhood of Kampung Attap, an area that has turned into a creative hub over the years – and one that the artist shows us around in the video. A cinematic experience: a visit to the German Film Museum, which is housed in a historic villa on the Museumsufer (© Uwe Dettmar/DFF; header image © Visitrhinemain/Udo Bernhart) The cinema of the German Film Institute and Film Museum: sophisticated works from all over the world are shown here (© Uwe Dettmar/DFF) Half-timbered houses and cobblestones: old Sachsenhausen is particularly idyllic in the Große Rittergasse (© Getty Images) At Schweizer Straße, not far from here, the flair is incredibly international. Japanese, Vietnamese and many other international restaurants line the street. Freshly rolled sushi is available at Sushiko and delicious pizza at A Tavola! , an Italian restaurant where you feel like you're sitting in an old Italian lady's living room. For me, the culinary diversity of the neighbourhood reflects the colourful and cosmopolitan Frankfurt that I like so much. The heart of the neighbourhood is Schweizer Platz with its flower shops, butchers and the Konditorei und Confiserie Georg Jamin. This is where my parents used to buy my birthday cakes. Until today the small family business still makes the best chocolates in town. If you walk towards it, you'll get to the Museumsufer am Main, where many different museums are located in old patrician houses. My favourites are the Deutsche Filminstitut & Filmmuseum and the Städel Museum which shows artworks from the last 700 years. From here, it's not far to Alt-Sachsenhausen with its small cobbled streets. Here I like to call it a day with friends in one of the many pubs, a glass of Apfelwein – the local cider – in hand.
