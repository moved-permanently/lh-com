["Celebrity chef Johann Lafer visits ham producer Vulcano in Styria to explore the artisanal craftsmanship behind some of the delicacies served in Lufthansa Business Class.","Lufthansa is rolling out a comprehensive quality initiative to make the travel experience even more exclusive for guests in every cabin. For its Business Class menu, the airline is drawing on the expertise of celebrity chef Johann Lafer and exceptional producers such as Vulcano, an artisanal company that transforms pork into fine delicacies in the volcanic region of Styria in Austria. Lufthansa Insights joined Lafer as he saw for himself just how much craftsmanship and time goes into every single piece The fire in the volcanoes of southeast Styria died out many millions of years ago. What they left behind was a rolling landscape that local people gradually transformed into a rich cultural region – a patchwork of orchards and small forests, vineyards, and fertile fields where pumpkins, runner beans, and various grains thrive. Many of the people living in Styrian Vulkanland (Volcanic Land) make their living from farming, tourism, and hospitality – and they are deeply passionate about good flavor and the quality of their products. Franz and Laura Habel are no exception. The father and daughter team runs the Vulcano artisanal production facility together. Visitors arriving at the company’s site in the quiet village of Auersbach near Feldbach are greeted at the entrance by a giant wooden leg of pork. The sculpture leaves little doubt as to what this place is all about: ham. More precisely, air-dried prosciutto – but also other pork delicacies such as lightly smoked pork neck, finely marbled collar, hearty bacon, and salamis flavored with either truffle or pumpkin seeds. Visiting Styria's volcanic region: Johann Lafer (left) and Christoph Brandstätter (center) visit Franz Habel at the Vulcano ham production facility (all images © Elias Holzknecht) Franz and Laura Habel inspect one of the air-dried hams that Vulcano is known for Time, craftsmanship, and careful aging give Vulcano's specialties their tender texture and rich flavor On this day in early December, the Habels are expecting important guests: celebrity chef Johann Lafer and Christoph Brandstätter, executive chef at Lufthansa caterer Gate Gourmet. The two chefs selected Vulcano as a supplier for Lufthansa Business Class and now want to take a closer look at the production process. The welcome at the courtyard restaurant, where visitors can sample Vulcano products, is warm. For Lafer, who was born in Styria, the visit is something of a home game. Brandstätter, too, is originally from Austria. “We are constantly scouting for new products,” says Brandstätter. “We want to keep evolving Lufthansa’s culinary offering. And Johann is a great partner in that process. He knows people and we know people.” Even so, the search is a real challenge, as Lafer adds: “Our goal is to find producers who can meet the volumes Lufthansa needs while still having a real person and a real soul behind what they do.” That is unquestionably the case at Vulcano. Franz Habel says he has already fed 200 pigs today. In fact, he is in the barn every single day for his inspection rounds, and vacations are rare. Franz and Laura Habel drive their guests personally to the production facility a few miles away, where pork is turned into delicious specialties. Once everyone has put on hairnets and sterile shoe covers, the tour begins – and it quickly becomes clear that everything here is still done by hand. In an enormous tub, two employees are salting 600 kilograms (1,323 lb) of pork neck, which will later be served on plates in Lufthansa Business Class. “For every batch, we can still tell you whether it was salted by Jakob or by Sepp,” says Franz Habel. Along with sea salt, they use herbs and spices such as coriander, pepper, and juniper. The exact recipe, of course, remains a secret. It takes around four and a half months for the pork neck to be finished – from salting and curing to gentle smoking over beechwood. “You’ve got to be in pretty good shape for that,” Lafer jokes to three women in a small room who are rubbing heavy hams with a paste made of fat, salt, and pepper in the areas not protected by the rind. Overall, the man who once earned two Michelin stars comes across just as cheerful and enthusiastic as audiences know him from television. “Incredible – I could stay here all day,” Lafer calls out to his colleague Brandstätter at one point. And “Mega!” escapes his lips several times as Franz and Laura Habel open one heavy metal door after another, revealing hams, slabs of bacon, and sausages hanging in seemingly endless rows on metal racks. For Johann Lafer, the product always comes first – which is why he seeks out exceptional producers in person “My philosophy is that the product is the star of the show.” Johann Lafer What makes Vulcano’s products so special comes down to one thing above all: time. “Look at the fine marbling in this meat,” Lafer says as he examines a ham. “You only get that when the pig has been allowed to grow slowly.” That, he says, is why Vulcano ham is so tender and juicy. Time also plays a key role in the curing process – as does temperature. “The hams here are hung at below 18 degrees Celsius (64 degrees Fahrenheit),” Lafer explains. “And the longer a ham matures at a cool temperature, the more intense the flavor becomes.” The goal, Laura Habel says, is for the fat to remain white. “And firm, but still delicate.” Why do Lafer and Brandstätter go to such lengths in their search for the best ingredients for Lufthansa Business Class – traveling across Europe and tasting products in person? “Because my philosophy is that the product is the star of the show,” Lafer replies. “On an airplane, you can’t cook in the same complicated, à la minute way you can in a restaurant. But if the core ingredients are outstanding, you can still create something truly special – something the crew is proud to serve.” Ready to discover the world? Claudia Hummel: I find it absolutely fascinating to see the world without borders. When viewed from the air, you realize that we are all one. I especially love flying over the Himalayas or the Alps. Over 5,000 pilots work at Lufthansa, so it’s rare for the same team to be in the cockpit. How do the captain and first officer coordinate with each other before takeoff? And what exactly do they discuss? Frederik Niedner: We meet at the Flight Operations Center two hours before departure for a briefing and to review the flight documents. This is a relatively time-consuming process and builds on the preparation we’ve already done at home. We discuss the latest weather information, any irregularities along the flight route and at the destination airport, and after about 30 minutes, we decide together how much fuel to load. This always depends on the weather and if we expect any delays on approach. We then consult with the cabin crew and board the aircraft together one hour before departure. By then, ideally, we’re already a good team.
