["Discover the best activities in Venice: from enjoying cicchetti and St. Mark’s Square to vaporetto rides, island excursions, and views of the lagoon.","Here are our three tips for an inspiring stay in Venice. The lagoon city is one of the few places in the world that feels instantly familiar yet constantly reveals new facets. Amid waterways, stone façades, and a history of trade, a city emerges that has lost none of its appeal over the centuries Few other things are as synonymous with Venice as cicchetti. At Cantina Do Mori, one of the city’s most traditional bàcari near Rialto, these Venetian snacks are served in the classic style with a glass of wine, known here as an ombra; a Crodino is a perfect non-alcoholic alternative, capturing the spirit of the aperitivo ritual without alcohol. Cicchetti usually consist of slices of bread (crostini) topped with polenta, seafood, meat, or vegetables, and are often enjoyed standing up. Because the focus here is not on a long meal but on the enjoyable experience of moving from bar to bar, dining in Venice takes on a pleasantly informal atmosphere. (© Mauritius; header image © Getty Images) More Culinary Delights: Risi e bisi: Somewhere between risotto and soup, Risi e bisi is a creamy rice dish made from Vialone Nano rice and peas. It is a classic in Venice and the surrounding Veneto region, and is often topped with Parmesan and butter. Baccalà mantecato: Baccalà mantecato is one of Venice’s best-known specialties: a finely blended cream of salted cod, usually served on bread or white polenta. It is particularly interesting to note that the fish does not traditionally come from the lagoon, but arrived in Venice as dried cod from Norway – a quiet reminder of just how far the city’s trade networks once stretched. Risotto al nero di seppia: Rice has always played an important role in Venetian cuisine, as the region is one of Italy’s major rice-growing areas. Accordingly, risotto al nero di seppia is also one of Venice’s best-known dishes: the squid ink dyes the rice a deep black and gives it its characteristic sea flavor. As is so often the case in Italy, there is no single definitive recipe here either. Sometimes tomato sauce is added, sometimes it is left out; some cook the risotto with garlic, while others deliberately avoid seasonings to allow the flavor of the sea to shine through. Piazza San Marco is far more than just Venice’s most famous square. With St. Mark’s Basilica, the Doge’s Palace, the Campanile, the Museo Correr, the Archaeological Museum, and the Torre dell’Orologio, the city’s political, religious, and cultural history converges here within a compact space. At the same time, it is Venice’s only true piazza – all other squares are called campi or campielli – which underscores its special status within the city’s fabric. Opening out onto the lagoon, St. Mark’s Square still appears today as a grand stage on which Venice reveals itself in all its grandeur. (© Getty Images) Also Worth Seeing: Rialto Bridge and Market: The Rialto Bridge is the most famous of Venice’s approximately 400 bridges and connects San Marco with San Polo at one of the city’s most historically significant points. Until the 19th century, it was the only permanent crossing over the Grand Canal and was thus situated exactly where trade, transport, and everyday life converged. The present stone structure by Antonio da Ponte was built at the end of the 16th century as a bold bridge spanning a single marble arch. With its rows of shops and sweeping views across the Grand Canal, the Rialto Bridge remains one of the city’s most impressive sights to this day. Especially in the evening hours, when the light reflects off the water, it exudes that special atmosphere for which Venice is famous. Cannaregio: Alongside the many smaller islands that form part of the city of Venice, the city consists of six larger districts (sestiere). Cannaregio is one of them. Here, the lagoon city reveals a quieter, more everyday side. In the former Jewish quarter around the Ghetto Vecchio and the Campo di Ghetto Nuovo, strikingly tall residential buildings with five or six stories still stand today – built due to a lack of space at a time when the Jewish population was only permitted to settle here. With its canals, small shops, and quieter alleyways, Cannaregio thus feels like a part of Venice where history is still immediately visible in the cityscape. Peggy Guggenheim Collection: The Peggy Guggenheim Collection is one of the most important museums of 20th-century art in Italy and is situated directly on the Grand Canal in the Palazzo Venier dei Leoni, the collector’s former residence. On display here are works of European and North American modern art, complemented by a sculpture garden and temporary exhibitions. Venice is best experienced from the water. A trip by vaporetto (water bus) or gondola across the Grand Canal and through the smaller canals completely shifts your perspective: suddenly, water gates, courtyards, and palace façades come into view that are barely visible from land. It is in this gliding motion that you realize the city does not sit on the water, but has emerged from it. (© Shutterstock) More Experiences: View from the Campanile: Anyone wishing to see Venice from above will find the best view from the Campanile, which stands just under 100 meters (328 feet) tall. A lift takes visitors up to the viewing platform, from which the view opens out over St. Mark’s Square, the city’s rooftops, and far out across the lagoon. It is this transition from the bustling square to the tranquil expanse high above the city that makes the trip to the top one of the most impressive experiences in Venice. Boat tour to Murano, Burano, and Torcello: Beyond the city limits, the islands of the lagoon are a worthwhile destination. Murano is still renowned today for its centuries-old glassmaking tradition, Burano is home to its famous colorful buildings, and Torcello offers a relaxing blend of tranquillity and culture. This variety makes the boat tour one of the most beautiful excursions around the city. Teatro La Fenice: The Teatro La Fenice is one of Venice’s great cultural venues, and it is no coincidence that it bears this name: like a phoenix, the opera house has been rebuilt time and again following several fires. From the outside, it appears relatively understated, but inside unfolds that magnificent world of boxes in gold and red velvet for which Italian opera houses are famous. La Fenice can be experienced in various ways: while watching an opera or concert performance, on a self-guided tour with an audio guide, or as part of a guided tour.
